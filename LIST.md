# The list

Seventy-seven yes/no questions, in the order to ask them.

This is the list on its own. The reasoning, the entropy budget, the dependency register and
the three model reviews that corrected it are in [QUESTIONS.md](QUESTIONS.md).

**Read this first.** The list does not do what the original question asked. It gets *almost*
everyone to a unique answer pattern, not everyone. P(nobody in 8 billion collides) is about
0.57 under generous assumptions, and identical twins answer 60 of these 77 questions
identically no matter what. Section 9 of QUESTIONS.md explains why that is structural.

**Spelling rule for every name question:** use the spelling on your main government ID or
birth certificate. No Latin form? Transliterate phonetically. Ignore hyphens, apostrophes and
spaces. "First given name" and "family name" mean whichever your own culture treats as such.
When a question asks about a letter your name is too short to have, answer **no**. When you
genuinely do not know, answer **no** - never substitute a fact from another question.

## Order

Grouped by where the answer comes from, and within that by how much each block is worth, so
that stopping early still leaves you with the best questions you could have asked.

Everything you know without asking anyone comes first (1-25). Then the four blocks about your
parents' **dates**, which are worth more per question than their names (26-49). Then your
parents' **names** (50-61). Then the weaker material: extra letters, your address, siblings,
body (62-74). The last three are near-useless for the general population and are the only
questions that can separate identical twins, which is why they are there at all.

An earlier version put the parents' name blocks before the parents' date blocks. That cost up
to 2.00 bits at the worst truncation point. This order costs 0.34.

## If you only ask some of them

| Stop after | Bits | Narrows you to |
|-----------:|-----:|----------------|
| 13 | 12.8 | 1 in 7,000 |
| 25 | 24.7 | 1 in 27 million |
| 49 | 43.3 | 1 in 11 trillion |
| 61 | 54.4 | 1 in 24 million billion |
| 77 | 65.8 | everyone but a handful, and never identical twins |

Those are counts of answer patterns, not of people. With 8 billion people the last row still
leaves somewhere between a handful and a couple of hundred people sharing a pattern with
somebody, because real answers cluster. QUESTIONS.md section 5 has the honest range.
